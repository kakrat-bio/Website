Every founder learns the word "moat" before they have built anything worth defending. It arrives early — in the deck, in the first hire's first week, in the WhatsApp group where six people are deciding what the company is — as if naming a defensibility strategy were the same as possessing one.

The word itself is borrowed, and the borrowing is the problem. It comes from a man describing businesses that had already survived forty years: enterprises with decades of earnings, entrenched distribution, and brands your grandmother trusted. He was using it *descriptively*, as a way of explaining, after the fact, why a business that had not been killed had not been killed. Founders use it *prescriptively*, at month eleven, about a product used by two hundred people, one of whom is the founder's cousin.

That is a category error, and it is expensive, because it changes what you spend the next year doing.

## The four moats that usually aren't

**Network effects.** The real thing is specific: each additional user makes the product measurably better *for the existing users*, without anyone at the company doing anything. Most early companies have something else — a chart that goes up. Users arriving is growth. Users making the product better for each other is a network effect. If you cannot describe the mechanism in one sentence that does not contain the word "community," you probably have the first thing.

**Switching costs.** These are real, and they are almost always someone else's. Switching costs accrue where a customer has poured data, configuration and habit into your system over years. At eighteen months, your customers can leave on a Tuesday afternoon and most of them know it. A month-to-month contract is not a switching cost. A free trial is the opposite of one.

**Economies of scale.** Requires scale. This is the whole joke. You cannot have the advantages of volume before you have volume, and the period during which you do not yet have volume is precisely the period in which a larger competitor, if they cared, would be able to hurt you. Most early companies survive this window not through defensibility but through *irrelevance* — nobody large has noticed yet. Irrelevance is a wonderful thing. It is not a moat, and it expires without warning.

**Brand.** Brand is the moat people most want to claim, because it feels like something you can simply decide to have. In practice it is the slowest-accruing of all of them. Brand is what happens when a lot of people have had a consistent experience of you over a long time and can predict what you will do next. Three years of consistent behaviour is a start. A nice wordmark is not a start.

## The ones that are real, and boring

The defensibility that early companies do sometimes have tends to be unglamorous enough that nobody puts it on slide four.

A regulatory position — an approval, a licence, a clearance — that took two years and an eight-figure spend to obtain, and would take a competitor the same. A dataset that is not merely large but *not legally assemblable* by anyone else, because you have the consents and they do not. A supply relationship with the one vendor who can actually make the thing at the tolerance you need. Distribution you own rather than rent, which mostly means an audience that will hear from you without a platform's permission. Tacit process knowledge: the forty small things your people know about why the yield drops on Thursdays, none of which are written down, all of which took eighteen months and several ruined batches to learn.

Notice what these have in common. None are strategic postures. All are consequences of having done something difficult, slowly, that a competitor would also have to do slowly. That is what a moat is: not a clever position, but *accumulated inconvenience* pointed at the people behind you.

## Deep tech is the strange exception

This is where the standard advice — "you will not have a moat for years, stop worrying about it" — inverts, and where a lot of founders in labs get bad counsel from people whose instincts were formed on consumer apps.

In biotech, materials, and hard engineering, a genuine moat can exist on day one, in the form of a patent that covers a composition of matter, and a regulatory pathway that a competitor cannot shortcut even with more money. This is roughly the opposite of software, where patents are mostly decorative and the real defensibility arrives late, through scale and habit.

The consequence is a set of mirror-image mistakes. Software founders in deep tech under-invest in IP, because in their world it never mattered, and discover in year three that their core claim was disclosed at a conference by a co-founder who thought a poster session did not count. Deep-tech founders over-invest in it, treating a granted patent as though it were a business — which it is not, because a patent is a right to sue, and the ability to exercise that right is itself a function of having money, which is a function of having a business.

And both groups tend to forget the moat that matters most in a twelve-year development cycle: being the group that everyone in the field assumes will still exist in a decade. That one is built entirely out of not dying, publicly, for a long time.

## Speed is not a moat

The most common answer to "so what is your moat?" — delivered with a slightly defiant chin — is *we move faster than they do*.

Sometimes true. Occasionally decisive. Never a moat. Speed of iteration compounding against a specific, narrow problem is the closest thing most early companies have to an advantage, and it is better understood as a head start that has to be continuously re-earned. The distinguishing feature of a moat is that it keeps working while you sleep. Speed stops the moment you stop.

Which does not make it worthless. A head start used to accumulate one of the boring, real things above is how most moats actually get built. A head start used to grow faster is just a faster company.

## The honest version

The sentence to practise is: *we do not have a moat yet, and here is the sequence of things that could become one.*

It is a less comfortable sentence in a pitch, and it will lose you the occasional investor who wanted to hear the confident version. It is also true, and it has a useful side effect: naming the sequence forces you to name what you would have to do, in what order, for defensibility to exist at all. That is a plan. The other version is a vocabulary.

The crows all caw that they have a moat. Very few of them are wet.
