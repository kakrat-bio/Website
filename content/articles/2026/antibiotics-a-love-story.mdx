Every Indian family is in a long relationship with one antibiotic. In ours it is azithromycin.

She lives in the steel dabba, the tin every kitchen has, with the paracetamol, two expired cough syrups and a thermometer nobody trusts. Someone sneezes twice, and a voice from the kitchen says, "Take an Azee."

No prescription. No test. Three tablets over three days, like a short film with no interval.

My family is not unusual. Yours has a version of this too. It is a love story, and I am sorry to tell you how it ends.

## Love at first sight

In 1928 Alexander Fleming came back from his holiday to a messy lab. He had left a stack of culture plates on the bench instead of tidying up, and on one of them a mould was killing his bacteria. Science has never had a better excuse for not doing the dishes.

By the 1940s penicillin was saving soldiers who would otherwise have died of an infected scratch. Before antibiotics, a rose thorn could kill you. After them, we started doing heart surgery, hip replacements, chemotherapy and Caesareans as if infection had been cancelled.

It was the best match medicine ever made. The families approved. The horoscopes matched.

Even at the wedding, someone gave a warning. In his Nobel lecture in December 1945, Fleming said it was easy to make microbes resistant to penicillin in the lab by giving them too little to kill them, and that careless use could do the same thing inside people.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> Everyone clapped and went for dinner.

## Taking her for granted

Bacteria can divide every twenty minutes or so when conditions suit them. Give a few billion of them a drug and a handful will survive by luck. Those few become the parents of everyone who comes next. It is evolution at T20 speed, and every time we take the drug we run the selection again.

And we take it a lot. India is one of the largest consumers of antibiotics in the world.<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> We take them for colds and viral fevers, which antibiotics cannot touch. We buy them over the counter, although by law they need a prescription. Since 2014 the rules have been stricter still for two dozen newer antibiotics, and since 2016 a Health Ministry campaign has asked for a red line on their strips that means exactly that.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a>,<a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> Ask ten people what the red line is for. I'll wait.

We also fed them to chickens. Colistin is a drug doctors save for the very worst hospital infections, the ones nothing else will clear. For years it went into poultry feed to make the birds grow faster. India banned that only in 2019.<sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup>

We treated her the way you treat a spouse you are sure will never leave: worked hard, never thanked, and lent out to the neighbour's chickens.

## In defence of the uncle

Every resistance story has the same villain: the uncle who feels better on day three and stops the course. We have all lectured him. Some of us have made posters.

The case against him is thinner than we were told. In 2017 a group of British infection specialists argued in the BMJ that there is little good evidence that stopping early breeds resistance. The bigger risk, they said, comes from taking antibiotics you don't need, or for longer than you need them.<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> Other experts wrote back that the authors had gone too far.<sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> That argument is still going.

So, to be clear, this is not advice about your own tablets. Take what your doctor prescribed, for as long as your doctor said. My point is about who gets the blame. For decades we scolded the man who took too little, while the real damage was done by everyone taking too much. The sneeze that got an Azee. The chemist who became a part-time doctor. The chicken on a last-resort drug.

The uncle was the convenient suspect. The real culprit was the whole baraat, the entire wedding procession, band included.

## How it ends

In 2024 a large study in The Lancet did the accounts. Infections that drugs could no longer treat directly killed about 11 lakh people (1.1 million) around the world in 2021. If nothing changes, the study forecasts 3.9 crore deaths (39 million) between 2025 and 2050. The region it expects to be hit hardest is South Asia. That is us.<sup><a href="#ref-8">8</a></sup>

Why not just invent new antibiotics? Because the business is terrible. A good new antibiotic is one doctors lock away and use rarely, for a week at a time. A blood-pressure pill gets swallowed every day for thirty years. Guess which one investors prefer. In 2018 an American company called Achaogen got a new antibiotic approved. In 2019 it went bankrupt.<sup><a href="#ref-9">9</a></sup> Investors noticed.

So the old drugs are failing and the new ones are slow to come. During my PhD I grew skin cells and tissue in the lab. The nutrient broth they live in is usually spiked with a cocktail of antibiotics to keep bacteria and fungi out. Every so often a few hulk bacteria survive the cocktail anyway, and within days they take over the whole flask. The clear pink broth turns yellow, cloudy and opaque, as if the bacteria are showing us the future of our old antibiotics. That is what the end of a love story looks like in a biosafety lab.

## Couples counselling

Resistance usually gets told as a morality tale about careless patients. I think it is a story about access. In much of India an antibiotic costs less than the auto fare to the clinic and takes two minutes at the chemist. A doctor and a test can cost a day's wages and a day's wait. People are not being foolish. They are taking the option we made easy.

So the fix is not one more poster telling the uncle to finish his strip. It is making the right thing as easy as the wrong one. A quick, cheap test that says "viral, go home". A chemist who can say no without losing the customer to the shop next door. And farms that cannot buy hospital drugs by the sack.

The marriage can still be saved. But like every marriage in trouble, it starts with one side admitting it stopped making an effort a long time ago. That side is us. Not the uncle.
