*A philosophical meditation on noise, chaos, and the eternal cawing of the human soul.*

There are words in every language that don't just describe the world — they are the world. In English, you have "serendipity." In Portuguese, "saudade." In German, "Gipfelschmaus" — *the specific joy of food tasting better after reaching a mountain peak.*

And then, sitting quietly in the vast treasure chest of the Gujarati language, wrapped in a shawl and sipping chai, is **Kakrat** — also whispered by certain elders as *Kaklat* — a word so perfectly engineered by the universe that one wonders if the cosmos itself coined it on a particularly loud Tuesday.

## So, What Is Kakrat?

Kakrat is, at its most literal, the harsh, discordant cawing of crows. The kaw-kaw-kaaaw of several opinionated birds who each believe, with absolute conviction, that they are making the most important point in the history of avian civilization.

But, and here is where Gujarati reveals itself to be a deeply philosophical language, the word escaped the birds long ago. It migrated. It evolved. It put on shoes and walked straight into every chaotic, overlapping, nobody-is-listening-but-everyone-is-talking situation known to humankind.

In short: Kakrat is noise with *ego*.

## The Philosophy of Kakrat

The ancient question of philosophy has always been: "If a tree falls in a forest and no one is around, does it make a sound?"

But the Gujarati question is far more practical: "If everyone in the room is talking at the same time, is anyone actually saying anything?"

This is the riddle of Kakrat.

Consider: every crow in a Kakrat believes it is the signal. None believe they are the noise. And is that not the very condition of modern human existence? We are all crows. We are all cawing. We are all, individually, convinced we are making perfect sense.

The philosopher Descartes said, "I think, therefore I am."

The Kakrat version is: "I caw, therefore everyone else is wrong."

## Kakrat in the Wild: A Field Guide

The naturalist in you will be pleased to know that Kakrat has several distinct habitats:

1. **The Family Dinner Table**

   This is Kakrat in its most evolved, most terrifying form. There are six conversations happening. Someone is defending a life choice made in 2009. Someone else is critiquing the dal. A child is crying for no reason anyone can identify. And Dadi is delivering a monologue that began before the food was served and shows no signs of concluding before dessert.

   This is not dinner. This is a symphony of Kakrat.

2. **The Wedding Mandap**

   The priest is chanting mantras. The DJ is doing a soundcheck. Two aunties are negotiating a marriage alliance for someone who has no idea this is happening. The groom is being given last-minute life advice by an uncle he has met twice. Sacred. Chaotic. Completely Kakrat.

3. **The Group Video Call With More Than Four People**

   Scientists have confirmed — and I am making this up, but it feels true — that the human brain loses all ability to take turns speaking the moment a video call exceeds four participants. What follows is pure, digital, buffering Kakrat. Someone is always on mute when they shouldn't be. Someone is never on mute when they should be.

4. **Your Own Brain at 2 AM**

   The most personal Kakrat of all. No crows required. Just you, your ceiling, and seventeen unresolved thoughts all insisting on being addressed simultaneously.

## What Kakrat Teaches Us

Here is the philosophical twist, the moment where the comedy bows and lets wisdom take the stage for just a moment:

Kakrat is not the enemy.

Silence, pure and absolute, is the sound of nothing happening, nothing living, nothing caring. Kakrat — maddening, overlapping, crow-brained Kakrat — is the sound of too much life in one place. Too many opinions. Too many feelings. Too many people who have somewhere to be and something to say.

The Stoics would tell you to seek stillness. The word Kakrat gently suggests that perhaps you should also learn to find your peace inside the noise — because the noise, my friend, is never going away.

Every family. Every meeting. Every wedding. Every WhatsApp group with 47 members. It is all Kakrat.

And somehow, inexplicably, we keep showing up. We keep adding our voice to the chorus. We become, willingly, yet another crow.

Perhaps because deep down, we understand:

A life with no Kakrat is a life no one cared enough to be loud about.

## In Conclusion

The next time a situation feels like a Kakrat, do not merely roll your eyes. Pause. Appreciate. You are witnessing one of the universe's oldest, most honest, most democratically chaotic phenomena.

Every crow thinks it's right.

Every voice thinks it matters.

And in the grand, ridiculous, beautiful orchestra of existence —

*maybe they all do.*

## A note on the word

Kakrat is used in Gujarati in the sense of loud commotion or discordant noise. Gujarati literary usage also connects the word directly with the clamour of crows; for example, the expression "કાગડાં કાકરાટ" appears in Gujarati writer Pinky Dalal's નષ્ટો મોહઃ.

The interpretation developed in this essay — "noise with ego" — is our playful philosophical extension of the word, rather than a formal dictionary definition.

Source: [Pinky Dalal, નષ્ટો મોહઃ, Gujarati literary text, Matrubharti](https://gujarati.matrubharti.com/book/read/content/19952611/lost-fascination-by-pinki-dalal)
