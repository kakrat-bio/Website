## If we could pick our problems

Let's be honest. Problems are not optional. Not in life, not in marriage, not in a housing society WhatsApp group, and definitely not in a nation of 1.4 billion people who all believe they could run the country better than whoever is running it.

So I am not going to wish for a problem-free India. That is a brochure, not a country. Instead, imagine some genie, or some government scheme with a very long acronym, came to us and said: "You can't escape problems. But you may choose them."

What would we pick? And, more importantly, what would we return to the counter, unopened?

People have asked this question before, in their own ways. In *The Matrix*, Agent Smith admits that the machines first built humans a perfect, painless world, and humans simply refused to accept it. Whole crops were lost. Apparently, we need something to complain about in order to feel alive. Every Indian uncle on a morning walk could have told the machines this for free.

## A father's wish, 1984

The Ambassador taxi smelled of diesel and agarbatti. Mehul bhai sat in the back seat, holding a cloth bag with two tiffins, a steel water bottle and an envelope of photographs nobody would look at again. Next to him sat his daughter, Pallavi, nineteen, going to Bombay to study. Bombay, not Mumbai. It was still allowed to be Bombay then.

At the station, the train was late, as was its constitutional right. Pallavi fidgeted with her dupatta. Mehul bhai wanted to give her a speech, the kind fathers rehearse for months. Instead he bought two cutting chais, handed her one, and said nothing for a long time.

Then, just as the whistle blew, he said it.

"Beta, I won't wish you a life without problems. That is a wish for a statue. I wish you just enough."

"Just enough what, Pappa?"

"Just enough suffering to make you strong, but never so much that it breaks you. Just enough hurdles that you have to invent a new way around them, but never so many that you stop walking. Just enough sadness that you cry once in a while, so your heart stays soft. Never so much that your eyes dry up and you stop feeling anything at all."

Pallavi laughed, because she was nineteen and thought this was very filmy. She boarded the train. He stood on the platform waving until the last coach was a dot, and then for some time after that, waving at the dot.

Forty years later, Pallavi is a grandmother. She has survived a hostel mess, two recessions, one bad boss, three good ones, and the invention of the group video call. She still cries at old Doordarshan songs. She is not hard. She is strong. Those are different things, and her father knew it on a railway platform in 1984.

If you have read the old forwarded story *"I Wish You Enough"*, where a parent at an airport says goodbye with a similar blessing, you'll recognise the spirit. Some wishes are universal. They just taste better with cutting chai.

## The problems we have now (please take them back)

Here is today's menu, arranged the way a good thali should be: the heavy stuff first, the dessert at the end.

**The ones that can actually kill us**

1. Air that you can chew. Delhi in November is not a city, it is a hookah with traffic lights.
2. Water that runs out in summer and floods the city in monsoon. Sometimes the same week. Bengaluru once had both, and nobody was surprised.
3. Heat waves that make 45°C sound like a mild day.
4. Malnutrition sitting a few kilometres away from food-delivery apps promising ten-minute biryani.
5. Hospitals where the queue is the treatment plan, and medicines that are cheap only on paper.
6. Antibiotics we pop like Hajmola, breeding bacteria that will outlive us all.

**The ones that slowly eat the nation**

1. Jobs for the degrees, and degrees for the jobs, rarely meeting in the same room.
2. The brain drain, where our best minds leave and send back WhatsApp forwards on patriotism.
3. Courts with cases older than the lawyers arguing them.
4. Corruption so normalised we call it "speed money", as if it were a fitness supplement.
5. Caste, religion and language arguments that we keep reheating like yesterday's dal.
6. A big gap between the India of airports and the India of bus stands.

**The ones that only ruin your mood**

1. Potholes with their own PIN codes.
2. The neighbour's wedding DJ, who believes the bass is a human right.
3. Honking at a red light, as if the signal will turn green out of embarrassment.
4. Government office forms that need a photocopy of the photocopy.
5. "Sir, your call is important to us." It is not.

**The samosa-and-chai tier**

1. The samosa that is all potato and no masala.
2. Chai that went from ₹5 to ₹20, but the cup got smaller.
3. The office debate on whether vada pav is better than samosa. (It has never been settled. It never will be.)
4. Uncle in the family group posting "Good Morning" with a sunflower at 4:47 am.
5. Someone eating the last Parle-G and putting the empty packet back in the dabba.

## The problems I'd like to have in 2050

Now the fun part. If problems are compulsory, let them at least be the problems of a rich, curious, slightly over-confident country. Here is my order, placed in advance.

1. **Sky traffic on Outer Ring Road.** Bengaluru has so many flying cars that there is now a jam at 300 feet above Silk Board. The commute still takes two hours. Some traditions must be preserved.
2. **A homesick mammoth.** The woolly mammoth, de-extincted in a US lab and gifted to Vantara, is having health issues because Gujarat is too warm. A committee of vets, climate scientists and one very confused astrologer is studying the matter.
3. **Children who are too comfortable.** Parents complain that AI has made life so easy their kids cannot handle the real world. The problem is, the real world is also fully automated now. Nobody can find any "real world" left to send them to, so summer camps teach "How to Wait in a Queue" as heritage training.
4. **Separatist moon miners.** The Indian colonies on the Moon and Mars, tired of exporting precious metals to Earth at government-fixed rates, demand a separate nation. Their slogan: "No Taxation Without Atmosphere." Parliament debates it for six weeks and then forms a committee.
5. **Too many Nobel Prizes.** Indian labs win so many that the western world is complaining "match fixing" and accusing a wealthy INSA lobby of pressuring the Nobel committee.
6. **Clean-air complaints.** People in Delhi complain the air is so clean that they can no longer feel the city. Nostalgia cafes sell "2019 Diwali smog" in sealed jars.
7. **The tourist overflow.** Foreigners queue for visas to India the way we once queued for US visas. Our consulates complain about the paperwork. We are, very privately, delighted.
8. **A shortage of problems for start-ups.** Every big problem is solved, so founders pitch apps to optimise the angle of the evening chai pour using laser governed AI hand.
9. **The cricket crisis.** India wins so often that people stop watching. The BCCI asks the team to lose occasionally, "for the content".
10. **Robots unionise.** Domestic robots demand a weekly off and new batteries as Diwali bonus. Indian mothers negotiate them down within ten minutes.

## So where will India actually be?

Here is the funny thing about us. We are proud of a golden age none of us saw. We are angry about a colonial loot that 99% of living Indians never experienced. And we argue loudly about a superpower future none of us has visited yet. We are a nation that lives mostly in the past and the future, and only visits the present to pay electricity bills.

Yet the present is exactly where 2050 is being built. Not by the kings in our textbooks or the viceroys we love to blame, but by today's software engineer stuck at Silk Board, the farmer checking soil data on a cracked phone, the PhD student running qPCR at midnight, and the auntie who runs a tiffin service with better logistics than most airlines.

My honest guess? India in 2050 will be neither the paradise of the optimists nor the disaster of the critics. It will be richer, louder, older, hotter and, I hope, cleaner. Many of today's big problems will shrink into small ones. Some new, strange ones will arrive, ones we cannot even imagine now, just as nobody in 1984 imagined "my data pack ran out" as a crisis.

The real test is not whether we become a superpower. It is whether our problems move up the list, from "the air is killing us" to "the mammoth is sweating".

## Just enough

Maybe Mehul bhai's wish on that railway platform is the best wish for a country too.

Not a perfect India. A perfect India would be boring, and worse, it would make our intelligence unemployed. A nation with nothing left to fix would slowly forget why it bothered to think.

I wish for an India with just enough problems. Enough to keep us inventing, arguing, building and fixing. Enough to make us cry once in a while, so we stay human. Never so many that our eyes dry up and we stop caring.

A little traffic in the sky. A sweaty mammoth. A moon colony with an attitude. A samosa with slightly too much potato.

That would be enough. That would be plenty.
