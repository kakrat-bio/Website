Last October, I was buying a lehenga online late at night. On the payment screen, an option popped up to pay in 15 days with zero interest. My salary was a week away, so I tapped it and went to sleep.

Ten days later, a text reminded me that my payment was due. It came with a link to something called a Key Fact Statement, and I opened it for the first time. The money hadn't come from the shopping app, or from my digital wallet. It had been lent to me by an NBFC I had never dealt with, and the PDF came with a neat table of late fees and interest rates.

So I went back and read the fine print properly. I'll use one app to show what I found: LazyPay, and its Pay Later credit, called LazyPlus. Other apps charge different fees and rates, and all of these can change. The figures here come from the lender's own disclosures as of April 2026.

## Who actually lent me the money

When you tap Pay Later on LazyPay, the lender is PayU Finance India Private Limited, a non-banking finance company (NBFC) registered with the RBI.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a>,<a href="#ref-2">2</a></sup> The app is operated by LazyPay Private Limited, another PayU group company, which acts as what the RBI calls a lending service provider.<sup><a href="#ref-2">2</a>,<a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Think of it as the shop counter. The money comes from the back office.

Why does it matter who's who? Because the RBI's rules are written for the lender. Under its Digital Lending Directions, the lender stays fully responsible for everything the app does on its behalf, from how the terms are shown to you to how you're reminded to pay.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> The lender can't point at the app and walk away.

The same rules give you two other protections. Before you borrow, you must get a Key Fact Statement: a short summary of what the loan costs. The signed loan papers, including the KFS, must also be sent to your registered email or phone.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> And your credit limit can't quietly go up on its own. You have to ask for it, and the lender has to assess and record that request.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup>

## What ₹10,000 can cost

I'm using ₹10,000 because it's the starting credit limit LazyPay advertises on its App Store listing.<sup><a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup> Say you spend all of it during the sales. There are three ways this can go.

**You pay the full bill on time.** The app describes repayment every 15 or 30 days as interest-free, and the lender lists no processing fee for this one-shot "bullet" repayment.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a>,<a href="#ref-5">5</a></sup> So if you clear the bill by the due date, it should cost you nothing extra. One catch: the lender's disclosure also lists interest of up to 39% a year on this credit, and says the rate for bullet repayments is fixed for the loan's tenure, without saying when it applies.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> Your Key Fact Statement is where to check.

**You turn it into EMIs.** Now there's a processing fee of up to 4%, which is ₹400, and interest of 14% to 29% a year.<sup><a href="#ref-1">1</a></sup> The lender's fee schedule says its fees don't include GST, so add 18% on the fee: another ₹72.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Spread over three months:

- at 14% a year, you pay about ₹10,706 in all;
- at 29% a year, you pay about ₹10,959.

That's ₹706 to ₹959 on top of the price, or roughly 7% to 10%.<sup>*</sup> If the sale gave you 10% off, the EMI can take most of that discount back.

**You miss the due date.** On the 15-day billing cycle, the late fee is up to ₹50 a day, GST included.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Five days late costs ₹250. Nineteen days late costs ₹950, about what the most expensive three-month EMI would have cost you. The fee keeps adding up for about two months, until it reaches its cap of 25% of what you owe (before GST). On ₹10,000, that cap is ₹2,500, or about ₹2,950 with GST.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

The 30-day billing cycle works differently. You pay one late charge based on your total bill, and for anything above ₹7,500 that charge is ₹700, GST included.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup> Separately, if your bank declines an auto-debit, there's a bounce charge of up to ₹500 plus tax each time.<sup><a href="#ref-3">3</a></sup>

## The button looks the same. The product may not be

"Buy Now, Pay Later" is the label on a button. Behind it there can be a credit card, a bank loan, or, as in my case, a loan from an NBFC arranged through an app. Which rules protect you depends on what the credit actually is.

If you're converting a credit card purchase into EMIs, the RBI's credit card rules apply. The card issuer has to show you the principal, the interest and any discount before you convert, and show them again on your statement. An EMI that carries interest can't be sold to you as "no-cost" or "zero-interest".<sup><a href="#ref-6">6</a></sup> If it's a loan from a bank or NBFC through an app, the Digital Lending Directions apply instead, with the protections above.

The quickest way to tell which one you have is to look at whose name is on the bill.

## It goes on your credit report

This was the part I least expected. The RBI requires lenders to report loans made through digital lending apps to credit bureaus, whatever their size or length. That includes short-term credit and deferred payments offered at a shop's checkout.<sup><a href="#ref-4">4</a></sup> A Pay Later bill can sit on your credit record next to your other loans.

That works in both directions. Paying on time builds a repayment history. Paying late leaves a mark: CIBIL says lenders specifically check for defaults and overdue amounts when they decide whether to lend to you.<sup><a href="#ref-7">7</a></sup> There's no fixed number of points you lose for one late payment, because it depends on the rest of your record. But the next time you apply for a credit card or a home loan, someone will read it.

## Before you tap

Pay Later is useful. A free fortnight of credit genuinely helps when your salary is a week away. The trouble starts when it feels like a discount and you stop treating it as a loan.

Before you tap, find four things: who is lending, what it costs in rupees, when it's due, and what happens if you're late. All four are in the Key Fact Statement, which you're meant to get before you borrow. It took me ten days to open mine.
