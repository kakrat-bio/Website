## A Brief, Respectable Introduction

I am a biologist, so let me start the way we biologists usually do, by making a simple thing sound terrifying.

According to my field, ageing begins with cells that can divide only a fixed number of times before they retire<sup>1</sup>, and it is driven by twelve hallmarks<sup>2,3</sup>, including genomic instability, telomere attrition, loss of proteostasis, disabled macroautophagy and deregulated nutrient sensing through the insulin/IGF-1 and mTOR pathways<sup>4,5</sup>, made worse by p16Ink4a-positive senescent cells<sup>6</sup> and something called inflammaging<sup>7</sup>, all of which can be measured with DNA methylation clocks built on 353 CpG sites<sup>8–10</sup> that reflect a loss of epigenetic information<sup>11</sup>, which OSK-mediated reprogramming can partly reverse<sup>12</sup>.

If you understood all of that, you probably need a holiday. If you didn't, don't worry, you are perfectly normal. In simple words, our cells slowly get worn out, messy and a bit forgetful, and scientists have built very expensive machines to measure exactly how messy they have become.

But with all that vocabulary at my disposal, not one of those papers explains why my favourite shirt has started fitting like a polite request.

## Meanwhile, in the Mirror

The textbooks describe ageing one molecule at a time. Real life shows it to you all at once, in the bathroom mirror at 7 in the morning, under a tube light that has never been kind to anyone.

For me it started with a single white hair. I plucked it, as tradition demands, and felt rather pleased with myself. Three weeks later it was back, and it had brought relatives. The biology behind this is actually quite lovely. Hair turns grey when the stem cells that supply colour to each hair root stop renewing themselves<sup>13</sup>, and newer work shows that many of them simply get stuck in the wrong part of the hair root, unable to move to where they are needed<sup>14</sup>. Stuck, immobile and unproductive in middle age: I know the feeling. In mice, sudden stress makes it worse, because nerves near the hair root release a burst of noradrenaline that uses up these colour-making stem cells for good<sup>15</sup>. So yes, that quarterly review meeting did leave a mark, several of them in fact, near the temples.

Then the hairline begins its slow retreat, like a political party losing ground one ward at a time. The cruel joke is that the same male hormones that thin the hair on your head also thicken your beard and body hair, and how each hair root responds depends entirely on where it happens to live<sup>16</sup>. That is how a man ends up with a strange redistribution scheme in his forties: less hair where people used to admire it, more of it in the ears and nostrils, and one heroic eyebrow hair, an inch long, curling upward like an antenna looking for better network.

Fat does something similar. It quietly leaves the places where it might have been useful and settles around the belly, mostly as the deeper fat around the organs, and this can happen even when the number on the weighing scale hardly moves<sup>17</sup>. Before you blame your metabolism, though, you should know that a study measuring daily energy use in people from 8 days to 95 years old found that it stays essentially flat between about 20 and 60, once you adjust for body size<sup>18</sup>. So it isn't your metabolism. It is the second helping of *rasmalai* at your cousin's wedding. Science has taken away our favourite excuse and given us nothing in return.

## The Mask of Adulthood

The changes in the body are at least honest about themselves. The stranger changes happen inside the head.

In my late teens and early twenties I had plenty of passion and very little wisdom, which, looking back, is exactly the right ratio for that age. Every problem had an obvious solution that the older generation was simply too timid to try. We would argue at the tea stall outside the hostel until 2 am about fixing the education system, Indian science and possibly the universe, and then walk back to our rooms convinced the job was more than half done.

Somewhere around thirty-five, the ratio starts to shift. The ideas are still there, but now they share space with EMIs, a manager, a parent's blood report forwarded on WhatsApp and a school admission form. I caught myself saying "let's see" in meetings, and worse, I finally understood why my father always said it. The molecules seem to agree that something happens around this age. When researchers measured nearly three thousand proteins in the blood of more than 4,000 people aged 18 to 95, they found that ageing doesn't move in a smooth line but in waves, and the first big wave arrives in the thirties<sup>19</sup>. Another study, which followed the same people over several years, found two major molecular shifts, one at around 44 and another at around 60<sup>20</sup>.

By forty-five you are close to the bottom of what economists call the happiness U-curve. Data from half a million people show that well-being dips to its lowest point in middle age and then climbs back up<sup>21</sup>, and a later analysis of 145 countries found the lowest point sitting at around 50, more or less everywhere on Earth<sup>22</sup>. So the midlife slump is not your personal failure. It is a statistically solid, internationally replicated, peer-reviewed slump.

Here is the part no review article will tell you: on the inside, nothing has changed. The twenty-year-old is still in there, ready to argue about the universe at 2 am, take an unplanned trip or sing badly in the car. Then I look around the table at a family function. Friends from my batch have picked up paunches, reading glasses and strong opinions on property rates, along with a particular way of sitting with their hands on their knees, as if a group photo might be taken at any moment. Their conversations have moved from "what if" to "what rate". Nobody says it out loud, but a quiet message goes round the table: *behave your age*. The child inside me reaches for the microphone, and I gently put him back in his seat.

So I wear the mask, first the mask of adulthood and then, slowly, what I can only call the mask of old-hood. What makes it so hard to take off is that we all enforce it on each other. Everyone wears one because everybody else seems to be wearing one.

And then, once in a while, there is an evening when the right four or five people end up at the same table, the ones who knew you before the mask went on. Someone retells the story about the hostel warden, someone else does the impression, and the masks come off without anyone noticing when. For three hours nobody is age-appropriate and nobody minds. You drive home at midnight feeling twenty-two, and wake up the next morning with a back that is very clearly not twenty-two. Biology, as always, gets the last word.

## The Grand Conference on Living Forever

All of this brings me to my favourite event on the scientific calendar: the conference on healthy ageing, anti-ageing, longevity, or whatever the funding agencies have decided to call it this year.

Imagine a ballroom in a five-star hotel, with chandeliers hanging overhead. If the conference is in India, it opens with the traditional lamp lighting, where three dignitaries take turns trying to light one stubborn wick while the photographer waits. The keynote has a title like "Reversing Biological Age: The Road Ahead". In the front row sit the patrons, wealthy men and women in their seventies and eighties who have funded the programme through philanthropy, and who have, in a very human way, a specific result in mind. They are not here for a modest improvement in the health of elderly mice. They are here for the pill, the one you take with breakfast and then live forever, healthy and, ideally, happy.

Behind them sit the scientists, and every scientist worth their salt in that room knows the real story. The posters in the corridor are beautiful. Mice on rapamycin live 14 percent longer if female and 9 percent longer if male<sup>5</sup>, worms live twice as long<sup>4</sup>, and epigenetic clocks get wound back a few years in a dish. All of it is real and important, and none of it is the pill.

The field has even said so in writing. In 2002, more than fifty ageing researchers signed a public statement warning that no anti-ageing product on the market had been shown to slow, stop or reverse human ageing<sup>23</sup>. The money, meanwhile, has never been more enthusiastic, and one rejuvenation company alone launched in 2022 with three billion dollars committed<sup>24</sup>. Then, in 2024, a study of the world's longest-lived populations concluded that radical life extension this century is unlikely unless we find a way to markedly slow biological ageing itself<sup>25</sup>.

So the conference runs on an unwritten agreement. Maybe ninety percent of the room privately believes the magic pill will not arrive within their patrons' lifetimes, and a full hundred percent have agreed never to say so, in quite those words, in front of the patrons. The slides say "promising" and the posters say "potential therapeutic target", but the coffee break says something else entirely, in lower voices, near the *samosas*. It is the most polite silence in all of science, and since it pays for a great deal of excellent basic research, nobody is in any hurry to break it.

The patron, for his part, goes home happy. He has seen the future, and it came with very good error bars.

## The Fine Print in Our Favour

Still, ageing is not only about losing things.

Our mental abilities don't all peak at the same time. Some peak around the end of school, some level off in early adulthood and begin to decline in our thirties, and some don't peak until our forties or later<sup>26</sup>. You may forget where you kept the car keys, but you now have exactly the right word to describe how annoying that is.

Psychologists have also described something called the positivity effect. As people get older, they pay more attention to positive information than to negative and remember more of it, apparently because they care more about managing their emotions and have become better at it<sup>27</sup>. In everyday terms, you stop fighting with strangers on the internet. You still read their comments, you just don't reply any more.

Psychiatrists have even tried to work out the brain biology of wisdom. Their proposal is that it depends on a balance between the thinking front of the brain and its older emotional parts, with dopamine and serotonin acting as referees<sup>28</sup>. Put simply, the part of you that wants to throw the phone finally starts losing arguments to the part that remembers what the phone cost.

And the happiness curve, remember, is a U. After the low point, it climbs back up<sup>21,22</sup>.

There are other benefits that nobody bothers to publish. You are allowed to leave a party at 10 pm, and you can say "I don't know" in a meeting without your heart rate going up. Your opinion of a film no longer needs anyone's approval. And you finally understand most of what your parents were telling you all those years, a superpower that arrives, rather cruelly, just after the point when they would have most enjoyed hearing you admit it.

## Epilogue: The Klotho–Melatonin Axis of Eventual Wisdom

As promised, I will end where every respectable biology story must end, with a pathway. Allow me to propose the one that actually governs a human life. With full grant-application seriousness, I call it the Klotho–Melatonin Axis of Eventual Wisdom.

It starts with a protein. In 1997 a Japanese team found a gene that, when broken in mice, produced something very much like human ageing: a short life, hardened arteries, thin skin, weak bones and damaged lungs<sup>29</sup>. They named it *klotho*, after Clotho, the Greek goddess of fate who spins the thread of life. When mice are made to produce extra klotho, they live longer, and it turns out the protein travels through the blood like a hormone, dampening the same insulin-like signalling that let those worms live twice as long<sup>4,30</sup>. In my model, klotho is the protein that decides this will be a long assay, not because the results are spectacular but because nobody has the heart to stop the incubation.

Wisdom, in this framework, is a gene that switches on only late in life. It stays silent through your twenties, however hard you try to switch it on, and it needs repeated exposure to one very specific trigger: being wrong in public, several times. Here biology hands satire a gift. People who carry a version of the *KLOTHO* gene that raises klotho in their blood tend to do better on tests of thinking and memory, and mice engineered to make extra klotho learn and remember better too<sup>31</sup>. So the long-life protein turns out to be a brain protein as well. I rest my case before any reviewer can object.

Finally, there is the hormone. Melatonin, made by the pineal gland deep in the brain, is the body's evening announcement, released when the eyes report that the light is fading. One study of melatonin in saliva found that its daily rhythm drops noticeably from around the age of 40, with middle-aged people showing only about 60 percent of the rise seen in the young<sup>32</sup>. Another careful study found no decline at all in healthy older adults<sup>33</sup>, because no hormone is ever allowed a simple story. Either way, the announcement seems to come a little earlier every year, at 9:40 pm, halfway through a film you chose yourself or in the middle of a conversation you were enjoying. The eyes send the message, and for once the brain doesn't argue.

So that is the whole pathway. Klotho spins the thread and keeps the assay running, wisdom switches on late after enough exposures, and one evening a hormone quietly taps the eyes and tells the brain it is time to sleep now. There is nothing alarming about it. It is more like a lab technician switching off the lights at the end of a long and mostly successful day, after checking that someone has labelled the tubes.

Until then, there are still evenings left when nobody has to be age-appropriate. I recommend attending every one of them. The data, frankly, support it.
