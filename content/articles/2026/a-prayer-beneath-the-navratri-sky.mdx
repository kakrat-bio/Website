That night, the air was full of the scent of raatrani, the night-blooming jasmine, and a faint smell of rain, as if the monsoon were saying goodbye. From the temple, the dhol and nagara drums and the bells of the Goddess's aarti carried all the way to our home. Strings of lights made the sky look different that night. It felt as if everything around us was waiting for the Goddess to arrive.

It was the first of the nine nights of Navratri in Jamnagar, my hometown in Gujarat, around 2015. Young girls in traditional dress danced the sheri garba, our neighbourhood's street garba, for the Goddess, to the beat of the drums and the notes of the shehnai. The adults danced with us, or offered seva, helping in whatever way they could.

My friend and I danced the whole night. We were exhausted, but neither of us wanted it to end. At eight, staying up for garba felt like an adventure. As we stepped down from the stage, clutching our lahni, the small gifts people give girls during Navratri as the Goddess's blessing, two of the women who had taught us the steps said, "That was wonderful! Eight more nights to go."

We went to sit in the empty room next door and wait to be collected. The snacks served as prasad tasted divine, whether it was ice cream, samosas or wafers. One by one, the other girls left. We kept our eyes on the door, waiting for our names to be called.

## Nobody came

Nobody came for us. When only the two of us were left, the organisers and the other didis went home. They told the caretaker to send us off when our families arrived. Our parents came every year, so they assumed someone would turn up this time too. It was a serious oversight. We were eight years old, it was one in the morning, and we were alone with a man we didn't know.

The caretaker offered to take us home himself. Our mothers had told us never to go anywhere with a stranger, or accept food from one, so we could not take a single step. He asked again and again, and again we said no. In the end he went to telephone the lost-children desk at the venue.

The lights began to blur through our tears. The garba music that had delighted us all night now felt sharp and piercing. Feeling helpless, we clutched our lahni in one hand and each other's hand in the other. We looked up at the sky, and at the Goddess in the mandap, and prayed without a sound.

## Found

While the caretaker was outside on the phone, two women noticed us. They were our neighbours, friends of my mother who knew us and the organisers well. They spoke to the caretaker, reassured us and led us away, carrying our bags of snacks and teasing us gently until we laughed. Then they got in touch with the organisers to find our mothers.

We found them at the street corner, completely lost in the sheri garba. When we stood right in front of them, they suddenly remembered: in the rush of the festival, they had forgotten to pick us up. Our tear-filled eyes said everything. For us, it was relief. For them, it became a lifelong regret. We thanked the two women who had brought us back.

## Everyone and no one

It turned out to be a simple mix-up. Two people each thought the other had checked on the last children, and both had already left. During the nine nights, different groups of women and volunteers took turns looking after the little girls, and in a crowd that big, with everyone busy with something, it was easy for a child to be missed. From the next night on, the organisers made sure every girl was accounted for before anyone left.

It taught my friend and me something too, even at eight. For the rest of Navratri, we made a point of telling the didi in charge when our parents had not yet arrived. The responsibility became shared: the adults learned to be more watchful, and we learned to speak up instead of waiting quietly to be noticed.

When everyone is watching the children, it is easy for no one to be watching them.
