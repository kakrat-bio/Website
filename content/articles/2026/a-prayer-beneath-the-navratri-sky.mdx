The raatrani was in bloom that night. Its scent hung so thick over the lane that you could almost lean on it, and beneath it, faint as a promise, lay the smell of rain. The monsoon was leaving, but slowly, the way guests leave in Gujarat. From the temple came the dhol and the nagara and the bells of the Goddess's aarti, carried over the rooftops all the way to our door. Someone had strung lights across the sky, and the sky had agreed to be different for nine nights.

It was the first night of Navratri in Jamnagar, my hometown, sometime around 2015. I was eight. The sheri garba, our street's own garba, had begun, and the little girls of the neighbourhood whirled in our chaniya cholis, their tiny mirrors catching the light, while the shehnai rose and fell above the drums. The grown-ups danced beside us or busied themselves with seva: a tray of prasad here, a tangle of wires there, a child's dupatta retied for the third time.

![Illustration of little girls in bright chaniya cholis dancing garba in a circle around a glowing clay garbo lamp, under strings of festival lights on a neighbourhood street at night](2026/a-prayer-beneath-the-navratri-sky/garba)

My friend and I danced until our feet no longer felt like ours. We were exhausted, and we did not want it to end. At eight, staying up for garba is not a late night. It is an expedition. When we finally climbed down from the stage, clutching our lahni, the small gifts girls receive during Navratri as the Goddess's blessing, two of the women who had taught us the steps beamed at us. "That was wonderful," one of them said. "Eight more nights to go."

We were sent to wait in the empty room next door until someone came to collect us. The prasad there tasted like nothing I have eaten since, though it was only ice cream, samosas and wafers. Blessed food does that when you are eight. Every few minutes the door opened, a name was called, and a girl ran out into the light. We kept our eyes on that door.

## The door that stayed shut

Little by little the room emptied, until there were only two of us. The organisers and the didis who had looked after us all evening gathered their things. Our parents came every year, so surely they would come this year too. They left us with the caretaker and asked him to send us home when our families arrived. Two of them, we learned later, each believed the other had checked that we would be collected. Neither had.

It was one o'clock in the morning. We were eight years old, and we were alone with a man we did not know.

![Illustration of two little girls seen from behind, sitting on a bench in an empty room with their gift bags beside them, watching a closed door with a thin line of light under it; a wall clock shows one o'clock](2026/a-prayer-beneath-the-navratri-sky/waiting)

He offered to walk us home himself. But our mothers had given us one rule, repeated so often it had worn smooth: never go anywhere with a stranger, and never take food from one. So we stayed exactly where we were. He asked again. We shook our heads. He asked once more, and we only held our lahni tighter. At last he stepped outside to telephone the lost-children desk at the venue.

That was when the night turned. The lights we had danced under began to swim, then blur, behind a film of tears. The garba music, so joyful an hour before, now came through the walls thin and sharp, like something that had forgotten us. We stood with our bags of gifts in one hand and each other's hand in the other, and looked up, past the strings of bulbs, to the Goddess in the mandap. We did not say anything. I am not sure we had words for what we wanted. We just prayed.

## Found

The answer came walking past the door. While the caretaker was outside on the phone, two women noticed two small girls standing very straight and very still, and stopped. They were our neighbours, friends of my mother who knew us well and knew the organisers too. One of them bent down and said our names, and the world became ordinary again. They had a word with the caretaker, took our bags from us as though they were precious, and walked us out, teasing us gently all the way, until somewhere between the stage and the street we laughed. Then they reached the organisers and went looking for our mothers.

We found them at the street corner, lost in the sheri garba, spinning with the other women. We walked up and stood right in front of them. It took a moment. Then I watched their faces change as they remembered: in the rush and joy of the first night, they had forgotten to come for us. Our wet eyes said everything there was to say. For us, the relief was so large it felt like tiredness. For them, it became the kind of regret a mother carries for the rest of her life. We thanked the two women who had brought us back. I am not sure we thanked them enough.

## Everyone, and no one

Nobody had been careless, exactly. Over the nine nights, different groups of women and volunteers took turns watching over the little girls. Some kept an eye on us themselves; others shared the task among friends. In a crowd that big, with everyone busy and happy and needed somewhere, it was easy for one child, or two, to slip between assumptions. From the next night on, the organisers made sure every girl was counted and collected before anyone went home.

We changed too. For the rest of that Navratri, my friend and I made a point of finding the didi in charge and telling her plainly that our parents had not come yet. The responsibility had become shared. The adults learned to look twice, and we learned that waiting quietly to be noticed is not the same as being safe.

I think of that night every Navratri, when the raatrani blooms and the first drums begin. I think of the two women who stopped, and of the prayer two small girls said without words. And I remember what it taught me, long before I could have put it into a sentence:

When everyone is watching the children, it is easy for no one to be watching them.
