Sneha found the diary while looking for a bedsheet.

It was tucked inside the old teak cupboard in her grandmother's room, under two silk sarees and a bundle of newspapers gone soft at the edges.

The house had been closed for six months.

Even so, it still carried traces of Savithri Paati. The faint smell of sandalwood powder. Coconut oil. Dried jasmine. In the kitchen, a steel tumbler still sat upside down near the sink, exactly where she used to keep it.

Outside, somewhere beyond the compound wall, a temple bell rang. A milkman argued with someone at the end of the street.

Sneha sat cross-legged on the red oxide floor and untied the faded thread around the diary.

On the first page, in blue ink, was written:

**Savithri. 1967. Private.**

She smiled.

She had never thought of Paati as someone who kept a diary.

Paati had seemed too practical for secrets. She stored tamarind in old Horlicks bottles, reused plastic covers until the print faded, and believed most family problems could be solved with food and silence.

Sneha turned the page.

The writing was entirely in Tamil.

She stared at it for a while, as though determination alone might make the letters rearrange themselves.

It did not.

She could speak Tamil easily enough. She could quarrel in it, joke in it, get scolded in it.

Reading was different.

Still, she kept turning the pages.

A few dates were circled in red. One sentence appeared again and again, underlined twice.

On the last written page, there was only a single line.

Under it, someone had drawn a small key.

Sneha took the diary downstairs.

Her mother was at the stove, spreading dosa batter in slow circles.

"Amma, read this for me."

Lalitha turned.

The moment she saw the diary, something in her face tightened.

"Where did you find that?"

"In Paati's cupboard."

"Put it back."

Sneha leaned against the kitchen counter.

"Why?"

"It was hers."

"She's dead, Amma."

Lalitha looked at her.

"Dead people can still have private things."

It was said quietly, which made it stranger.

Sneha opened the diary.

"What does this line mean?"

Her mother glanced at the underlined sentence.

For a second, she looked almost uneasy.

"Nothing important."

"That's not what I asked."

"Go eat before the dosa becomes cold."

And that was that.

Later, in the backyard, Sneha handed the diary to her cousin Karthik.

He was sitting under the mango tree with a cup of coffee in one hand and his phone in the other.

"Can you read Paati's handwriting?"

Karthik took the diary reluctantly.

"I can read Tamil."

"Good."

"I said I can read Tamil. I didn't say I enjoy challenges."

He bent over the page.

His smile faded.

"What?"

He read a little more.

Then closed the book.

"No."

Sneha laughed.

"No what?"

"I am not getting involved."

"In what?"

"That is exactly what I don't want to find out."

He handed it back and walked inside.

By then, curiosity had settled firmly in her.

That evening, she went to see Raghavan Thatha, Paati's younger brother.

He lived three streets away, in a house crowded with newspapers, medicine strips and framed photographs of people Sneha only vaguely remembered.

A devotional programme was playing loudly on television.

She showed him a photograph of the sentence.

He looked at it for a long time.

"What does it say?"

Raghavan Thatha lowered the volume.

"He came again tonight."

Sneha looked at him.

"Who came?"

He did not answer.

She showed him the last page.

"And this?"

His lips moved as he read.

"If anything happens to me, the truth is under the third stone."

Sneha felt a small chill despite the evening heat.

"Which stone?"

"I don't know."

"You do."

The old man looked towards the window.

"Some things are better left alone."

The sentence might have sounded foolish from anyone else.

From him, it did not.

That night, rain began after midnight.

It drummed softly on the tiled roof, then harder.

Sneha woke to a sound downstairs.

Knock.

She lay still.

Another knock.

Then silence.

The electricity went out.

She sat up.

For a few seconds she considered waking her mother. Instead, she switched on the torch on her phone and went downstairs.

The back door was open.

Just a little.

Rainwater had blown onto the kitchen floor.

Outside, near the old well, were three flat stones set into the earth.

Sneha stood there looking at them.

Then she went closer.

The third stone shifted when she pulled at it.

Underneath was a rusted metal box.

She crouched beside it, rain hitting her shoulders.

A shadow moved behind her.

Sneha turned and screamed.

The shadow screamed louder.

"AYYO!"

The power returned.

Her mother stood in the doorway, clutching a steel tumbler.

"Amma!"

"Why are you shouting?"

"Why are you standing there in the dark?"

"I came for water!"

"At one-thirty?"

Lalitha looked ready to argue.

Then she noticed the box.

Her expression changed.

"You found it."

Sneha stopped.

"You knew it was there?"

The next morning, the family gathered in the hall.

Word had travelled faster than Sneha understood.

Karthik came. Raghavan Thatha came. Two aunties arrived carrying snacks. Shanthi Mami from next door appeared too, saying she had come to return a bowl.

Nobody remembered lending her one.

The box was opened in the middle of the floor.

Inside were old letters, photographs, a brass key and a small cloth pouch.

One photograph showed Savithri as a young woman, standing beside a man Sneha had never seen.

On the back were the words:

**To Savithri. Until we meet again. Ravi.**

No one spoke.

Raghavan Thatha opened the first letter.

Ravi had lived nearby.

Savithri had been nineteen.

They had fallen in love.

Her family had arranged her marriage to another man.

For months, Ravi had come to see her at night.

Sneha thought of the sentence in the diary.

*He came again tonight.*

There had been a plan to leave for Madras.

The key belonged to a railway locker. Inside, Ravi had kept money and two train tickets.

Savithri never went.

She stayed.

Years later, she married Sneha's grandfather.

The last letter was in Savithri's own handwriting.

Raghavan Thatha read it aloud.

"I kept these because my children know me only as their mother, and my grandchildren know me only as Paati. I wanted there to be something left of the girl I was before all that."

The room grew quiet.

Sneha looked at the photograph again.

The young woman in it did not look like Paati.

And yet she did.

There was the same straight back. The same serious eyes.

For the first time, Sneha understood that the old woman she had known had once stood somewhere between one life and another, not knowing which one she would choose.

Karthik picked up the cloth pouch.

"What's this?"

Inside was a folded scrap of paper.

Raghavan Thatha opened it.

He read.

Then he began to laugh.

Not politely. Properly.

Lalitha took the note from him.

She read it and started laughing too.

Sneha frowned.

"What does it say?"

Her mother wiped her eyes.

"It says, 'If my grandchildren find this and make a big drama, tell them Ravi became bald by thirty. I made the correct decision.'"

For a moment, nobody reacted.

Then the room broke into laughter.

Sneha looked at the photograph of Ravi.

He did, in fairness, have excellent hair.

"There's one more line," Raghavan Thatha said.

He read it aloud.

"And no, the gold is not in this box."

The laughter stopped.

One of the aunties looked towards the stairs.

Then another.

Karthik was already on his feet.

Shanthi Mami, who still had no reason to be there, asked, "Which cupboard did you find the diary in?"

Within seconds, the room emptied.

From upstairs came the sound of cupboard doors opening.

Then Lalitha's voice.

"Karthik, put that saree back!"

A few minutes later, someone shouted, "I found another key!"

Everybody rushed towards the sound.

"What does it open?"

A pause.

"The sewing box."

"And?"

"Twelve safety pins and three buttons."

Sneha sat alone in the hall, laughing.

She picked up the diary.

On the wall above her was an old photograph of Savithri Paati, older now, hair silver, expression severe.

Sneha looked at it for a while.

Then she closed the diary and placed it beside her.

Upstairs, the search for the missing gold had moved to the rice tins.
