For most of the history of biochemistry, a protein was something you found, not something you made. You could mutate one, trim it, fuse two together, express it in a bacterium and hope. But the deep grammar — how a chain of amino acids folds itself into a working machine in the time it takes to read this clause — stayed mostly opaque. You worked with what evolution had already written, the way a film editor works with footage someone else shot.

That changed faster than most people outside the field noticed, and it changed in a peculiar way: quietly, in a field that had spent thirty years being told it was about to change and wasn't.

## From prediction to design

The shift didn't start with design at all. It started with prediction: given a sequence, what shape does it fold into. That problem had been a punchline — a standing biennial competition where good entries were wrong in interesting ways — until it wasn't. Once prediction was tractable, the inverse became conceivable. Given a *shape you want*, what sequence would produce it.

That inversion is what generative design tools attempt, and increasingly deliver on at the bench rather than only in simulation. The current toolkit is roughly: a diffusion model proposes a backbone — the folded shape, without deciding which amino acids occupy it — and a second model designs a sequence likely to fold into that backbone. A structure predictor then acts as the referee, checking whether the proposed sequence would in fact produce the shape that was asked for. Three models, arranged in a loop, each covering for the others' failures.

The important thing about that loop is not that it is clever. It is that it is *free*. These tools are open-source and run on hardware a mid-sized lab already owns, which means the people exploring the design space are no longer only the four labs in the world with a decade of structural-biology tradition behind them.

## What "it works" actually means

Here is where most coverage goes wrong, in both directions.

The breathless version says proteins are now designable on demand. The dismissive version notes that most designs fail and concludes nothing has happened. Both are avoiding the actual number, which is more interesting than either.

BindCraft, a binder-design pipeline published in *Nature* in 2025, reports experimental success rates of **10 to 100 per cent**.

Sit with that range for a second, because the range *is* the finding. A tenfold spread is not a measurement problem or authorial hedging; it is the shape of the field. On a well-behaved target with an obvious pocket, you can now design binders essentially at will, one design giving one binder, without high-throughput screening or rounds of experimental optimisation. On a difficult target, you are back to throwing a lot of candidates at the wall. Which target is which is not always predictable in advance.

And "binder" is doing real work in that sentence. Designing a protein that sticks tightly and specifically to a chosen surface is a genuinely different problem from inventing a new catalytic mechanism from scratch. The first is now, on a good day, routine engineering. The second is not.

## The results that are hard to be cynical about

Even discounting appropriately, some of what came out of that work is difficult to wave away. Designed binders that reduce IgE binding to birch allergen in samples from actual patients. Binders that modulate the gene-editing activity of Cas9 — that is, a designed protein used as a dial on another protein's behaviour. Binders that redirect adeno-associated virus capsids toward particular cell-surface receptors, which is the unglamorous, load-bearing problem in gene delivery.

None of those are demonstrations that a molecule folded correctly. They are demonstrations that a molecule that has never existed did a specific job in a messy biological context, which is a much higher bar and the only one that matters.

## The honest ceiling

A 2026 review from David Baker's lab puts the current state about as plainly as a review ever does: the long-standing challenges of designing new protein structures, assemblies and binders are *close to being solved*, and the key questions in those areas are now "not how to design, but what to design."

That is a remarkable sentence, and it is also carefully bounded. What remains genuinely hard, by the same authors' account: catalysts for reactions with high energy barriers, and — more fundamentally — switches and nanomachines that integrate binding, conformational change and catalysis in one molecule. Which is to say: the things real proteins mostly are. Haemoglobin does not simply bind oxygen; it changes shape when it does, and that change is the entire point.

So the field has learned to design nouns, and is still working on verbs.

## What to watch next

Not benchmark numbers. The useful signal is how the failure modes evolve as targets get harder — whether difficulty turns out to be a smooth gradient the methods will grind through, or a wall with something categorical on the other side.

The other thing worth watching is what happens to the phrase "what to design," now that answering it no longer requires a decade of specialist training. For thirty years, the bottleneck in protein engineering was capability, and imagination was cheap because it was unusable. That ratio has inverted. A tool that is free, open and mostly works hands the interesting constraint back to whoever can think of something worth building.

Historically, biology has not been short of those people. It has been short of ways to let them try.
